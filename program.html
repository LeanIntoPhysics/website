Lean into Physics

The Program

I believe it’s incumbent on an educated person to know some physics. Two reasons.

First, it’s the closest to a recipe for the sinews of reality that we’ve got.

Second, the mental apparatus developed from the study—the building and responsible use of models—when properly transferred, is highly valuable in all structured disciplines, which is how physicists have been able to contribute far beyond physics (when they managed to overcome the field’s infamous hubris).

The way we teach physics today makes it hard three different ways.

First, we teach it in the order and style of its discovery. Research is famously a lot like looking for a black cat in a dark room. The room has long since been well-lit, but we still make students put on a blindfold and retrace the researchers’ steps: Newton, then Maxwell, then Lagrange, then Planck and Einstein, then (hopefully) Noether—and so on.

This is unnecessary. Physics as we conceptualize it today has a very elegant inner structure: states, what counts as the same state, laws, symmetries, and the conservation laws symmetries buy you. Taught from the start, it helps avoid a lot of unnecessary pain. This has been repeatedly recognized by better women and men than I. In fact, “Physics from Symmetry” tries to do just that (with a hat tip to its author Jakob Schwichtenberg, who seems to have been very similarly motivated).

Second, we teach physics either to engineers who need to calculate it, or to physicists who need to expand it. The former is already a multi-year lift just on the basics, and assumes a good deal of math being learned alongside (at least up to differential equations and linear algebra). The latter is a decade plus-long vocational training run aimed at an academic career. With a few noble exceptions of varying technicality, someone not aiming at either goal is left to choose between textbooks and pop-sci.

Third, which connects back to the first—we teach physics in Greek math. Yes, the only thing worse than math with Greeks is math without Greeks, and far be it from me to knock the notation that allowed humanity to do hard physics in the age of quills and horse-drawn postal carriages. That said, Greeks are not standardized, cryptic, ambiguous, and as anyone who’s done tensor index juggling can tell you, error-prone.
